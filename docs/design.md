## Problem Statement

Carbon-aware scheduling tools tell every flexible electricity user (EV fleets, data centers, office buildings) to shift load into the same "clean" hours. When many large loads follow the same signal, there is not enough spare clean generation, so fossil plants ramp up to serve the rush. Realized savings fall short of what the signal promised, and emissions can rise. Martin, Powell & Rajagopal (Nature Communications, 2025; verify volume/article number before it goes on a slide) showed this for EV charging on a Western US grid model and proposed "Cascading": one aggregator coordinates its EVs in smaller groups.

Open gap: coordination across *independent, competing* companies with different load types, and a way to pay each one for the emissions its participation in coordination actually avoided, beyond what it would have saved acting alone.

## What Makes This Cool

- One chart tells the story: total CO2 versus flexible load (% of regional peak), for four strategies. It shows the herding gap (sum of solo savings vs realized joint savings) and how much coordination recovers.
- It turns coordination into money: every participant gets an "avoided-carbon receipt" (bid, assigned hours, emissions vs the go-it-alone counterfactual, Shapley share, payout at a stated carbon price), settled on actual grid conditions.
- Framing: a carbon clearinghouse, not another scheduler. The hard part of paying for avoided emissions is the counterfactual; the calibrated simulator answers it, and the baseline is chosen so companies are paid only for what coordination adds.

## Premises (agreed)

1. The novel claim is coordination across competing companies plus payouts for avoided emissions. The herding insight is prior work; cite the Nature Communications paper.
2. Herding must show up in our own simulator before anything else gets built.
3. (Revised after second opinion) ML forecasts the future grid; a calibrated simulator answers the what-ifs. The forecast model predicts next-24h demand, wind and solar from real EIA-930 history. Carbon intensity is never an ML label taken from the simulator: the "average intensity" signal is computed from EIA-930 fuel mix x fuel emission factors (for training/eval) and, at plan time, by running the simulator on forecast inputs. No ML model is trained on simulator output.
4. Chalk serves forecasts and features on every clearing run. (Access is settled in hour 0, with a cutoff at hour 6; see Next Steps.)
5. Payouts use a visible, stated carbon price ($/ton) and a stated pool size.

Dropped: federated learning. The coordinator needs only flexibility bids. Privacy = data minimization: companies submit a bid, never usage history; in a real deployment each company sees only its own slot and receipt. The demo shows the operator/auditor view of all receipts, labeled as such.

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: Merit-order clearinghouse (CHOSEN engine)
One real region; merit-order simulator of real plants; Chalk-served forecasts; greedy coordinator; exact Shapley; receipts. Effort M, risk low. Main gap: no transmission congestion.

### Approach B: PyPSA network dispatch
Same clearinghouse with transmission limits and co-optimized dispatch. Most rigorous; effort L-XL, risk med-high for 24h. Not chosen.

### Approach C: Interactive flexibility exchange (CHOSEN demo, on A's engine)
A website where the user plays several made-up companies, submits their bids, and watches the coordinator clear them. Originally a stretch goal; promoted to the main demo by team decision. Multi-user live bidding from phones stays a stretch.

## Recommended Approach

Engine = Approach A. Demo = Approach C as a single-user website. The system splits cleanly into a **backend** (data, simulator, forecast, coordinator, settlement, API) and a **frontend** (bid builder, "watch the coordinator" view, results), joined by one JSON API contract agreed in hour 0.

## The Demo (what a judge sees)

1. **Story screen:** one-line thesis, the adoption chart, and a credibility strip (region, real day used, calibration error, data sources).
2. **Bid builder:** the user creates up to 8 made-up companies (presets: EV fleet, data center, office building, cold storage; or custom), each with energy (MWh), earliest start, deadline and max power (MW). A slider sets how much *other* flexible load in the region is already chasing the clean hour (the background population). Carbon price and payout pool are visible inputs.
3. **Watch the coordinator:** a 24-hour stacked load chart in three acts the user steps through:
   - Act 1, default: every company runs as soon as its window opens.
   - Act 2, everyone chases the clean hour: companies use the standard carbon-aware signal; loads pile into the same hours; the CO2 counter shows savings falling short of what was promised.
   - Act 3, coordinated: the coordinator places blocks of energy hour by hour (animated from the backend's placement trace); each hour shows its marginal resource (coal, gas, or curtailed renewables if modeled) and the CO2 counter updates.
4. **Results:** strategy comparison, the herding gap callout, and an avoided-carbon receipt per company (labeled operator view; in production each company sees only its own) with its Shapley share and payout.

Interactive features (all run on the already-trained forecast model and the ready coordinator; nothing is trained during the demo):

- **Day picker:** choose one of 3-4 curated real days (e.g., windy spring night, sunny mild day, summer heat peak, cold winter morning). Days are chosen by weather type before any results are seen, not picked for drama. The whole demo re-runs on the chosen day.
- **Plant on the margin (hover):** hovering any hour shows the real power plant your extra load turns on in that hour (eGRID plant name, fuel, CO2 rate), or "curtailed renewables" if modeled. Makes the abstract "marginal emissions" concrete.
- **Beat the coordinator:** before Act 3, the user drags their companies' energy blocks into hours by hand and gets a CO2 score. Then the coordinator runs and the two scores are compared side by side.
- **Leave the coalition:** a toggle per company removes it from coordination; total avoided CO2 and everyone else's Shapley payouts update instantly. Shows that payouts reflect each company's real contribution.

The "coordinator" is an optimizer, not an LLM. Call it the coordinator (or clearing engine) in the pitch.

## Backend

### B1. Region data + simulator

- Region: ERCOT recommended (largely islanded, so imports are small). CAISO is the alternative (stronger solar story, large imports).
- Inputs: EIA-930 hourly demand and generation by fuel; EPA eGRID plant capacity, heat rate, CO2 rate; EIA fuel prices. EPA CAMPD hourly unit data is optional extra validation.
- Dispatch rule per hour: residual demand = demand − (nuclear + hydro + wind + solar + net imports from EIA-930). Fill residual demand from the fossil stack sorted by marginal cost (heat rate x fuel price), with each plant's available capacity = nameplate x per-fuel availability factor. Vectorized via cumulative capacity + `searchsorted`.
- Curtailment: EIA-930 wind and solar are post-curtailment, so EIA-930 alone cannot reveal spare clean energy. In hour 1, try to get ERCOT curtailment or available wind/solar capacity data; if found, added load in a curtailed hour is first served by the curtailed MWh at 0 t/MWh. If not found, state plainly that clean-hour absorption is not modeled and that savings estimates are conservative.
- Fitted parameters (named, small): per-fuel availability factor, monthly fuel price scaling. Fit on training days only.
- Validation on held-out days, all reported (and served by the API for the credibility strip):
  (a) hourly coal vs gas generation split vs EIA-930 actuals;
  (b) marginal emission factor: regress ΔCO2 on Δfossil generation hour-to-hour in the data (EIA-930 fuel mix x emission factors, or CAMPD) and compare with the simulator's marginal rates;
  (c) hourly fossil CO2 error.
- Output: `emissions(load_profile) -> tons CO2` and per-hour marginal resource, including the marginal plant's eGRID name, fuel and CO2 rate (for the hover feature).
- Day set: 3-4 curated real days (by weather type, chosen before results), all held out from calibration fitting. If ERCOT Public API data is used, pick days on or after Dec 11, 2023 (its earliest date).

### B2. Forecast model (ML), served by Chalk

- Gradient-boosted model forecasting next-24h demand, wind and solar from EIA-930 history (features: lags, hour, day of week, rolling averages).
- Chalk surface (trimmed for 24h): point-in-time offline queries for leakage-free training data, windowed aggregation features, model serving called on every clear with latency returned in the API response. Bid features served as online features.
- Fallback: the same `get_forecast(region, as_of)` and `get_bid_features(bid)` signatures backed by a local implementation, so nothing is blocked on Chalk. The API reports which source was used.

### B3. Bids and strategies

- Bid model (all load types): interruptible energy E (MWh) to deliver within [earliest, deadline] at up to P_max (MW), on a single 24h horizon (midnight to midnight; no windows crossing midnight). Infeasible bids (E > P_max x window hours) are rejected with a clear error. Office HVAC pre-cooling and data-center batch work are approximated this way; stated on a slide.
- Bids are additional load on top of EIA-930 historical demand, under the same rule for every strategy.
- Sizing: ERCOT peaks in the tens of GW, so a single 100 MW company barely moves the margin. Presets use real-world reference sizes (published data-center and EV-depot sizes) and can represent aggregators (e.g., "EV fleet aggregator, 500 MW"). The background population (synthetic flexible load at a user-chosen % of peak, fixed seed) runs strategy 2 in every scenario: it represents everyone else already using a standard carbon-aware tool. It is fixed context, not part of the payout game.
- Planning vs scoring: every strategy plans from the same forecast inputs. Every strategy is scored by running the simulator on the actual day's EIA-930 inputs. The interactive demo uses a single day-ahead plan (no rolling re-plans).
- Strategies (applied to the user's companies):
  1. Default: each bid runs at P_max from its earliest start until E is met.
  2. Independent-average: each company fills the feasible hours with the lowest forecast average carbon intensity at P_max until E is met (ties: earliest hour), unaware of others. (The standard carbon-aware tool.)
  3. Independent-marginal: each company, alone, runs the same greedy as strategy 4 on only its own blocks, assuming the other user companies run default. Then all execute together. (Isolates the value of coordination from the value of a better signal.)
  4. Coordinated: blocks of 1% of each bid's energy, processed round-robin across bids in order of earliest deadline; each block goes to the feasible hour with the lowest incremental simulated emissions given all blocks already placed; ties broken by earliest hour. Emissions vs load is non-convex, so greedy is not guaranteed optimal; stated. Fallback check at planning time only, on the forecast: if greedy is worse than strategy 3 for a coalition on the forecast, use strategy 3's schedule. This guarantees 4 ≤ 3 only on the forecast, never on actuals; the API reports whether the fallback fired.
- Placement trace: strategy 4 records each block placement (bid, hour, MWh, incremental CO2), subsampled to ~200 steps, for the frontend animation.
- Herding metric (same model throughout): predicted savings = sum over companies of each company's solo savings vs default (that company shifts via strategy 3, the others at default). Realized savings = savings when all companies run strategy 3 together. The gap is the herding effect. The same comparison is computed for strategy 2.
- Comparisons: 2 vs 3 shows the value of a better signal; 3 vs 4 shows the value of coordination (the novel claim).

### B4. Adoption chart (precomputed)

- X-axis: flexible load as % of regional peak demand, using a population of many scaled synthetic bids (randomized windows and sizes from stated distributions, fixed seed, averaged over K draws with a variability band).
- Y-axis: total CO2 for the day, for strategies 1-4, scored on actuals, single day-ahead plan.
- Precomputed offline and served as JSON (it is too heavy to compute per request).

### B5. Settlement

- Baseline for payment (additionality): all user companies act alone with strategy 3. Companies are paid only for what coordination adds beyond what they could save alone. Savings vs default and vs strategy 2 are also returned, labeled separately.
- Coalition value v(S) = emissions(all run strategy 3) − emissions(members of S coordinated via strategy 4, non-members run strategy 3), scored on actuals, with the background population fixed. Strategy 3 for non-members is a conservative baseline (the best a company could do alone), which keeps payouts lower.
- Information: when planning for coalition S, the coordinator knows non-members' strategy-3 schedules and treats them as fixed. So v({i}) can be positive: it is labeled as value from reacting to others' known schedules, not coordination.
- Exact Shapley over all 2^n coalitions for n ≤ 8 (at most 256 coalition runs, each a single day-ahead plan). Above 8 bids (multi-user stretch only), Monte Carlo Shapley; the API reports the method.
- Schedules are fixed from the plan; settlement is computed after the fact on actual grid conditions.
- Payout = Shapley tons x carbon price. If total payouts exceed the pool, prorate. A negative share means $0 paid, shown with an explanation. Check that shares sum to v(full coalition).
- Leave-one-out: from the same 2^n coalition values (no extra simulation), compute for each company i the total avoided CO2 and the Shapley shares of the remaining companies in the game without i. Returned with the clear response so the leave-the-coalition toggle updates instantly.
- Gaming note (stated, not solved): bid windows are self-reported; a company could misstate them. Production would fix baselines from declared-in-advance or historical profiles.

Scored on actuals exactly like the strategies, with the other user companies at default so the comparison is with the coordinator's schedule under the same rules. Latency target under ~1 s.

## Future Directions (post-hackathon, not in the 24h scope)

These are for the pitch's long-term vision slide. None of them is built during the hackathon.

### 1. Business model: energy-operations automation for flexible-load businesses (from teammate discussion)

The clearinghouse is the wedge, not the whole business. The long-term product automates energy operations for businesses with flexible loads (EV fleets, data center batch jobs, building pre-cooling, cold storage, water pumping): the platform decides *when* their energy-heavy jobs run, so the business doesn't have to.

- **Who pays:** only the businesses using the platform (B2B subscription or a share of savings). Power producers are not charged; they are not customers.
- **Payouts to customers:** each business earns money back for the emissions its participation avoided, which gives it a reason to join and keep handing over flexibility (win for the customer, the platform and the climate).
- **Where payout money comes from:** outside sources, not the platform's own subscription revenue (paying customers out of their own fees is only a discount):
  - lower electricity bills (see section 2);
  - demand response payments from utilities and grid operators (today paid via aggregators such as Voltus and CPower);
  - avoided-emissions credits (voluntary carbon markets) and the value of corporate emissions reporting.
- **Platform revenue:** subscription plus a cut of those payments.
- **Considered and rejected:** general automation of businesses' internal and external tasks (grunt work, jobs). It is undifferentiated (crowded AI-agent/RPA market), requires knowing each customer's processes in detail, and doesn't connect to the grid problem unless the automated tasks use flexible energy. Automation stays limited to *when* energy-using jobs run.
- **Physical constraint to state in the pitch:** businesses can't receive power from a specific renewable plant; electricity flows through a shared grid. The levers are timing (what the clearinghouse does) and renewable contracts on paper.

### 2. Co-optimizing emissions and cost (electricity bill savings)

The hackathon coordinator minimizes emissions only, so bill savings are a side effect, not a guarantee. A future version optimizes emissions and cost together, using ERCOT's public price data.

- **How shifting lowers bills:**
  - time-of-use rates (cheaper off-peak hours);
  - real-time pricing (prices drop, sometimes below zero, when wind and solar are high);
  - demand charges (spreading load lowers a business's monthly peak).
- **Where it doesn't hold:**
  - businesses on flat rates save nothing by shifting;
  - clean and cheap hours don't always line up (some cheap night hours are fossil-heavy);
  - packing one company's load into fewer hours can raise its own peak and demand charge.
- **Pitch wording until this exists:** "can also lower electricity bills where customers pay time-of-use or real-time rates," not "will lower bills."

### 3. Auto-bidding with federated flexibility forecasting (uses each company's history)

In the hackathon, companies type their bids by hand. In production, bids would be generated from each company's own history, which is the concrete form of the "automate their operations" idea in section 1.

- **Auto-generated bids:** a model trained on a company's past operations predicts tomorrow's flexibility (e.g., when an EV fleet's vehicles return and how much charge they need, or a data center's batch queue) and submits the bid automatically.
- **Why federated learning fits here:** many companies have the same kind of prediction task, their histories are private (charging sessions reveal routes and operations), and each one's dataset is small while patterns are shared. Federated learning trains one shared flexibility forecaster per load type while each company's raw history stays on its own servers. (This differs from the coordinator, which needs only bids, which is why federated learning was dropped from the core design.)
- **Fairer, harder-to-game payouts:** history sets each company's baseline from past usage, as demand response programs already do, instead of trusting self-reported bid windows (the known gaming weakness in B5).
- **Reliability-aware scheduling:** history shows how reliably each company follows its schedule, so the coordinator can avoid depending heavily on companies that often miss their slots.
- **What it does not help:** the grid forecast (company loads are tiny next to regional demand) or the core novelty claim (federated forecasting of EV charging demand is already published).
- **Possible later demo:** Caltech's ACN-Data (real EV charging sessions from several sites; access likely needs a free token, verify in its docs). Treat each site as one company and train a federated model that predicts each site's next-day charging need and turns it into a bid. Estimated 4-6 hours; only after the core demo works.
